# Design Notes for Grayson

## How No Cause for Alarm Became No Cause for Alarm

I want to write this down because by the time we finish this campaign, there are going to be things I forget. Not the big stuff. I will probably remember Pepper killing Kaleb forever. What I am more likely to forget are the weird little turns along the way: the names that changed, the adventures I almost used, the ideas I was convinced were going to be important and then threw away, and the things you guys did at the table that made me rewrite what I thought the campaign was going to be.

So this is not the story of what happened *in* No Cause for Alarm. This is the story of how I built it, how I kept changing it, and how you and the other players changed it with me.

### The First Version: Osric and the Silent Vault

The oldest version of this campaign looked almost nothing like the version you are playing now.

The first character at the center of it was named **Osric**. I knew he became Milton Foal later, but when I went back through a reconstruction of the original prep, I recovered a detail I had left out of these notes: at one point his full name was **Osric Vale**. Osric and Milton were not two characters who were supposed to meet. Milton was what Osric became as I kept working on the campaign.

The first big location was called the **Silent Vault**. Eventually I was calling it the **Quiet Vault**. Even the way its magic worked changed while I was designing it. One early idea had stronger protections the deeper you went, including magic being suppressed in the lower parts. By the time I had a complete Session 1 plan, that had changed. The lower Authorization Hall allowed magic, and the upper section suppressed it.

The party was supposed to go into the Vault looking for **Tomas Reed**, a surveyor who had gone into an underground structure exposed by a storm. Milton had been stuck there for an absurd amount of time. Malthivar, not Milton, was the person the Vault had originally been built to contain.

Milton had one stupid-looking but important possession: Malthivar's old staff had been repurposed into an ordinary broom.

I liked that. Not because the broom was secretly going to turn back into the Staff of Ultimate Ass-Kicking or something. It mattered because Milton cared about it. It was not secretly storing Malthivar's consciousness. Sometimes a thing matters because of whose it used to be.

That is something I still like in NCFA now. An object does not need a stat block to matter. Sometimes an old broom is important because it is the last thing somebody has from another life.

By the end of that planned first session, the Vault would be destroyed. You would get Tomas out, and you would also bring Milton into the world. The idea was that what felt like a simple rescue would have consequences nobody at the table could possibly understand yet.

### The Session 1 I Almost Ran

I had a whole first session worked out for that version. It was not just a vague idea about a mysterious dungeon.

A huge storm and flood had hit Bellwether. Before anybody went exploring, I wanted the party to spend a little time rescuing people together. Six or so civilians, floodwater moving too fast, a runaway cart. Nothing that was supposed to kill you or turn into a giant skill-check exam. It was a way to make you a team before there was even a dungeon.

After that, **Mayor Jord Trollhorn** was supposed to send you to investigate what he called 'some kind of underground structure.' He did not know it was a magical prison. Tomas Reed's survey crew had exposed the entrance after the mudslide, and Tomas had ended up inside.

The first major fight was against the **Vault Wardens**. These were security constructs, not 'Custodians.' Their eyes would turn red, they would warn you that you were somewhere you were not authorized to be, and then they would attack.

The fight happened in the lower **Authorization Hall**, where spellcasting still worked. I remember that detail because the final version was actually pretty specific: I was not trying to surprise the spellcasters by turning their spells off halfway through the first combat.

Defeat the last Warden, and the Vault's ancient self-destruct starts. It was supposed to collapse immediately, but the machinery had deteriorated so badly that the place was falling apart in stages. Then, when you moved into the upper section, the magic suppression would kick in. Spellcasters would actually feel their power being pushed down.

You would meet **Milton before Tomas**. Milton was not a villain lurking behind the last door. He was a curious guy who had no idea how you had got inside. He knew where Tomas was hiding and wanted to help you find him. He also wanted you to take him with you.

Tomas was supposed to recognize how bad the structural damage was because he was a surveyor. Milton would recognize the security protocol because he had lived inside it for so long. Between them, you would understand one thing: run.

The session ended with everybody escaping through the same tunnel and the Quiet Vault being completely destroyed. The planned reward was twenty-five gold each and a free place to stay at Ledger House.

That version even had a specific building plan for Ledger House: I was using the Tarsakh Manor layout as its skeleton instead of making up a new floor plan.

None of this is a secret account of what happened at our table. It is the adventure I had prepared *before* the campaign became something else. That distinction matters because it is easy, later, to remember a plan as though it actually happened.

### Milton and Malthivar Were Not Just Prisoner and Jailer

There was more to Milton's time inside the Vault than I put in the first version of this letter.

Milton had been an employee of **Malthivar** before either of them ended up inside. Malthivar was the intended prisoner. Milton was there by accident, which I always thought was a much worse fate.

For something like fifty years, Malthivar treated Milton as his servant. Then Malthivar accepted that he was not getting out, got bored, and began teaching the one person he had to talk to.

They ended up with this uncomfortable teacher-and-student relationship that almost became friendship. Almost. Malthivar still thought he was better than Milton, even after centuries together.

About two hundred years before the campaign, Milton managed to make a spell work in a place that was supposed to stop magic. Malthivar died as a result. I never settled exactly how that happened, and I am not going to pretend I did.

After that, Milton was alone. He sometimes seemed to have Malthivar with him, but the plan was never that Malthivar's ghost was haunting him. It was what years of confinement and their relationship had done to Milton's head.

That made the broom better, I thought. It was ordinary and strange and sad. A piece of Malthivar survived, but not in the usual D&D way where it turns out to be a cursed talking artifact.

A lot of the tragedy in Milton depended on that. He was not created evil. He had been trapped, taught, hurt, and finally set free. The question was what he would do with all that freedom.

### What Milton Was Supposed to Become

Milton was not supposed to show up with a sign over his head that said VILLAIN.

The thing that defined him was **freedom**. After being trapped and controlled for so long, freedom had become basically sacred to him.

I had a more specific version of that idea in the original plan than I remembered. Milton hated the idea that Fate, or whatever you want to call it, gets to decide what somebody can become. He thought a person's options should not be decided by how much money they were born with, who their parents were, where they lived, or what education they were able to get.

That is a pretty noble thing to believe. I wanted him to be right about a lot of what was wrong with the world.

At first, that is easy to sympathize with. People should be free. Okay.

Then it starts moving a little. People need enough power to protect their freedom. Still pretty reasonable.

Then: If people cannot protect themselves, maybe somebody stronger needs to protect them. Now we are getting into dangerous territory.

And eventually: If people make choices that threaten their own freedom, maybe somebody else should make those choices for them.

That was where Milton was headed. I liked the idea that he could become a villain without ever abandoning his original belief. He would still tell you that everything he was doing was about freedom. He would just gradually redefine freedom until it meant Milton deciding what was best for everybody else.

He was supposed to spend a long time as somebody you liked. A person who fixed things, helped people, and slowly learned that he had astonishing magical power. The original outline aimed for the real reveal somewhere around level thirteen. He would even leave Bellwether after hearing Brakkus talk about the world beyond it.

The catastrophe at the end was supposed to be enormous. Thousands of people would eventually die, but I did not want to make Milton some guy who had simply decided to murder thousands of people. Most of the destruction came from other people copying, exploiting, or misusing what he had done, and from the consequences spiraling beyond his control. I wanted you to have to ask how much of that was his fault, and how much was yours for helping him along the way.

And I did not want a single required ending. You might have to kill him. You might stop him another way. You might even find a way to bring back the Milton you once knew. That was supposed to be your decision, not mine.

That idea did not disappear when Milton stopped being the main villain. There is still a lot of that DNA in NCFA: power, control, the excuses people make when they think they know better than everyone else, and the difference between helping someone and deciding for them.

### Before Perrin and Tyren, There Was the Harrow Collective

Something else almost became the backbone of the entire campaign, and it is missing from the earlier version of these notes: **the Harrow Collective**.

My plan was for you to spend a lot of the campaign thinking they were the main problem. They were a small group, maybe ten or twenty people, that collected dangerous artifacts, investigated magical phenomena, experimented on creatures, and tried to fix regional problems using methods that were getting harder and harder to justify.

The important part was that they were not supposed to be wrong about everything. Sometimes they really would solve a problem. Sometimes somebody would be better off because of something they did. And sometimes their experiments would be indefensible.

A man named **Calder Vane** was supposed to be the face of it. He was smart, charming, and capable of making an argument that sounded good until you stopped and thought about what he was asking people to pay for it.

I wanted defeating Calder and the Collective to be an actual victory. Not one of those endings where the DM immediately says, 'Ha! They were never important! You accomplished nothing!' They were a real threat, and beating them mattered.

The trick was that they were not the final threat. Milton, who might have spent years helping you fix things around town, was the person whose ideas would eventually cause the biggest disaster.

There was even a version where Calder could meet Milton and have no idea whom he was talking to. I liked that because it made the world feel less like everybody important already knew everybody else important.

I was trying to set up a campaign where the party could legitimately defeat one villain and still discover that the much more painful problem was somebody they cared about.

That was a solid idea. It was also part of a completely different campaign from the one we eventually played. I do not want to quietly stuff the Harrow Collective back into modern Bellwether and pretend they were secretly behind everything. They belong in the history of how I built NCFA.

### A Few Names That Never Made It to the Table

The people in that first plan changed too. It was not just Osric becoming Milton.

At one point I had **Inspector Mara Venn**, who was later renamed **Everlee Slade**, a tiefling. I had the old mayor, **Jord Trollhorn**, asking the party to investigate the structure. The names in the old prep were not all names that had earned a place in the version of Bellwether you know.

I also had a particular calendar year, 1495 DR, even though I deliberately never picked the exact day and month. I could tell you what year it was but not what Tuesday it was. That is pretty much how my prep works sometimes.

The names are worth writing down because they show the work. A character can go through two or three names before I figure out who they are. A location can change names and even move its important rooms around.

Not everything from that draft needed a modern equivalent. I did not want to save every old idea just because I had already typed it.

### I Knew the Kind of Campaign I Wanted Before I Knew the Campaign

Even back then, there were some things I knew I wanted.

I wanted you guys to have a place that felt like home. I wanted you to be able to leave, go do something dangerous, and come back.

I wanted that home to change. If you saved a neighborhood, I wanted people to move back into it. If a road became safe, I wanted merchants to start using it. If trade came back, I wanted money to start moving around. If money started moving around, I wanted somebody to get rich and somebody else to get pissed off.

I wanted the world to keep going while you were somewhere else. While you are in a dungeon, somebody is fixing a roof. Somebody is going to work. Somebody is putting their kid to bed. Somebody is getting into a bar fight. Somebody is cheating at cards. Somebody is having a really boring day. Most of those things will never become adventures. They are still happening.

That is one of the biggest things I want NCFA to feel like: **You are the center of the story. You are not the center of the world.**

If every weird thing in Bellwether turns out to be Tyren, the world gets smaller. If every old ruin is connected to Perrin, the world gets smaller. If every criminal is secretly part of the Iron Covenant, the world gets smaller. Sometimes a criminal is just a criminal. Sometimes two guys get drunk and fight. Sometimes a library is just a library.

That is why I decided not to turn the library into something like the Library of Alexandria. It did not need to secretly be one of the most important libraries that ever existed. It could just be important to Bellwether. That is enough.

### Looking for an Old Adventure That Already Did What I Wanted

At some point I realized I was trying to solve a campaign-structure problem that D&D had probably solved before.

This was going to be my first long campaign as a DM. I did not see any particular virtue in making my job harder just so I could say I had invented every single thing myself.

So I started digging through published adventures.

The question was not: What published campaign should I run?

The question was: **Is there an old campaign whose structure already matches what I am trying to build?**

If there was, I could steal the useful bones and build NCFA on top of them.

### Under Illefarn

One of the first serious possibilities was *Under Illefarn*.

There was a lot I liked about it. It was grounded. There was a community. There were older ruins beneath the current world. It worked at the lower levels where I wanted to start.

For a while I thought I might use it as the opening and then transition into something larger: use Under Illefarn to get everybody started, then move into the real long-term campaign structure.

I eventually dropped that. The more I changed the opening to fit what I wanted, the less reason there was to use Under Illefarn in the first place.

At some point you are no longer saving yourself work by adapting something. You are just doing extra work to make somebody else's story look like yours. That became a recurring lesson.

### Against the Slave Lords

I looked at *Against the Slave Lords* too.

There were things in that series I liked a lot. You have local problems that turn out to be part of something larger. There is an organized enemy. You can investigate your way upward through a network. There is a real sense that somebody behind the scenes is benefiting from all of this.

The problem was that once you make the Slave Lords the campaign backbone, the campaign becomes about finding and stopping the Slave Lords. The villains become the spine.

I wanted the **city** to be the spine. Bellwether still needed to matter even during stretches when nobody was thinking about the main villain.

So I kept ideas from that kind of structure, but not the structure itself.

### The Bloodstone Adventures

I also looked at the *Bloodstone* series.

That was attractive for almost the opposite reason. Those adventures get huge: settlements, armies, politics, territory, and high-level threats. The characters eventually become major figures in the world.

I liked that sense of escalation. I still do. I want there to be a point where you can look back and say: We used to care about one spider in a warehouse, and now look at the crap we are dealing with.

But Bloodstone starts too far up that ladder for what I wanted.

I wanted the small stuff to matter first. A warehouse should matter. A missing person should matter. Opening a road should matter. Helping one family should matter.

You guys should become important. You should not begin the campaign as people who already operate at the scale of armies and kingdoms.

So Bloodstone was interesting to look at, especially for what a campaign could become later, but it was not the right foundation.

### Princes of the Apocalypse

*Princes of the Apocalypse* was probably one of the more serious possibilities.

It had multiple factions. It had different directions you could go. It was less like following one hallway from Chapter 1 to Chapter 2 to Chapter 3. I liked that.

For a while, I could imagine using that kind of regional structure and then replacing pieces with my own material.

The problem was eventually obvious. If I used Princes of the Apocalypse as the backbone, NCFA would become the elemental cult campaign.

The cults would determine what mattered. The cult locations would determine where you went. The cult plot would determine the campaign's shape.

And I was getting more interested in Bellwether itself than I was in any particular enemy organization: who owns the rebuilt buildings, who gets rich when a district becomes safe, who gets pushed out, who controls the roads, what the Council does, what the churches do, and what ordinary people think.

Those questions mattered more to me than elemental temples. So that one went away too.

### The Anthology Books

I also looked at adventures in books like *Candlekeep Mysteries*, *Journeys Through the Radiant Citadel*, and *Quests from the Infinite Staircase*.

Those were interesting for a different reason. I did not really think one of those books was going to be NCFA. But I thought I might be able to take individual adventures and drop them into the campaign.

Maybe one gave me a good mystery. Maybe one gave me a weird location. Maybe one gave me something I could use at a particular level.

That idea never completely went away. I still do not care where a good idea comes from. If an old adventure has a really good map, I can use the map. If it has a cool setup, I can steal the setup. If somebody wrote a great monster encounter forty years ago, there is no prize for pretending I did not see it.

The important thing is that once it comes into NCFA, it needs to feel like it belongs here. I never wanted it to feel like: Okay, this week we are visiting somebody else's campaign for three hours.

### Then I Found Ruins of Adventure

Eventually I found *Ruins of Adventure*, the AD&D module connected to *Pool of Radiance*.

That one clicked almost immediately.

It had the thing I had been trying to build. A city had been mostly lost. A small part had been reclaimed. Beyond the safe area were dangerous districts. Adventurers could take jobs, push into those districts, deal with whatever was there, and gradually expand the part of the city that was usable again.

As the city was reclaimed, the situation changed.

That was basically the skeleton I had been looking for.

So this was the biggest early pivot in NCFA. I stopped trying to invent the entire campaign structure from zero and started using *Ruins of Adventure* as a **chassis**.

That word is important. Chassis. Not story. Not canon. Not a script. A chassis. It gave me a shape I could build on.

### The Loop I Really Wanted

What I really took from Ruins of Adventure was the campaign loop:

- You come home.
- You see what changed.
- You hear about several problems.
- You pick something to do.
- You leave the safe part of the city.
- You do something dangerous.
- You change the situation.
- Then you come back.
- And now home is a little different because of what you did.

That is still one of the basic rhythms of NCFA.

Even now that the campaign has moved much farther away from Ruins of Adventure, I still really like that loop.

The city is not just the place where you buy potions between adventures. The city is part of the adventure.

### Bellwether Starts Replacing Phlan

The more I worked on it, the less interested I became in simply converting Phlan.

Bellwether started becoming its own place.

The Council became its own thing. The neighborhoods started having their own problems. The factions became more specific. The politics became more specific. The reclamation started creating consequences that were not really part of the old module at all.

And then there was Ledger House.

### Where Brakkus Came From

Brakkus was not created for NCFA.

You already knew that, but someday you might forget how much of him came from somewhere else.

**Brakkus Ledgerhelm was one of my characters at Hall of Heroes.**

I played him there every week for about four months, from around level 3 to level 10.

By the time I put him into this campaign, I had already spent a lot of hours being Brakkus.

That meant I knew him differently than I knew most NPCs. I knew how he reacted when somebody pissed him off. I knew when he would be patient. I knew when he would decide somebody was full of shit. I knew the things he took seriously. I knew the kind of humor he had. I knew what he thought a paladin was supposed to be.

That made him useful when I needed somebody to run the place that was supposed to feel like home.

But I had one big rule for myself: **Brakkus could not still be the hero.**

He had been my player character. Now I was the DM. This was not going to become a campaign where my old character follows you guys around being cooler than everybody. That would suck.

So I changed him.

### Turning My Character Into Your NPC

The Brakkus you know at Ledger House is an older version of the Brakkus I played.

I did not throw away his Hall of Heroes history. I treated it like the earlier part of his life.

He adventured. He got older. He got much more powerful. And eventually, he stopped.

By NCFA, he is around forty-eight, a retired Oath of Devotion paladin of Torm, and the owner of Ledger House.

I expanded his history to fit that older version. His father was **Korgak**, an Ironfang warlord. His mother was **Elena**, a captive librarian who taught him to read in secret and told him stories about Torm. The clan treated Brakkus terribly and called him 'Runt.' Elena died from neglect when he was sixteen. Brakkus eventually fled and chose the name Ledgerhelm to honor the mother who taught him that there was more to life than his father's clan.

Later, a High Justiciar named **Vance Halford** became his mentor and helped him find his place as an Oath of Devotion paladin. Brakkus's holy symbol was the worn cover of Elena's ledger, marked with Torm's silver gauntlet. His sword, **The Appendix**, was an old archivist-paladin's blade with tiny runes that glowed gold. That is a very Brakkus weapon, and I still like it.

The detail that mattered most to me was that Brakkus believed honor came from what you chose to do, not what family you had been born into. Looking at the original Milton notes now, I can see why I planned for Brakkus and Milton to be a contrast. Both thought choice mattered. One was learning to help people make their own choices. The other was eventually going to convince himself he had to make those choices for them.

A lot of that helped me answer the question: How did the adventurer I played become this guy behind the bar?

But the most important change was his job in the story.

At Hall, Brakkus was one of the protagonists. In NCFA, he is not.

He can be stronger than you. He can know things you do not know. He can have friends in places you have never been. If somebody comes into Ledger House looking to hurt people, they may discover that retirement does not mean helplessness.

But Brakkus is not supposed to go solve the campaign for you.

He gives you a home. He gives me a character I already understand. And then he gets out of the way.

**Brakkus was mine first. Bellwether is yours.**

### Then You Brought Me Jarrock

Jarrock is probably the most interesting example of one of your ideas changing what I was building.

You did not make Jarrock for NCFA. You brought him in from **Jill's backstory**. Jill was already one of your characters from your Outschool campaign.

The strange thing about that was that we already knew something important about Jarrock's future.

Chronologically, that future has not happened yet. The Jarrock you are playing in NCFA is younger than the Jarrock from Jill's backstory.

But we already know that one day Jarrock kills Jill's parents, the King and Queen, and then takes Jill in.

That gave me a really interesting problem.

I did not want that future to happen just because somebody had written: Jarrock kills the King and Queen and takes Jill.

I wanted it to make sense.

I wanted us to someday be able to look at the Jarrock you are playing now and say: Yeah. I can see how that guy became the guy who did that.

There are two completely different parts to that. Why does Jarrock eventually kill the King and Queen? And why does the guy who just killed their parents take their kid home with him?

Those both need an answer.

### Jarrock Helped Create One of the Big Themes of NCFA

This is where the theme of **power corrupting people** really started becoming important to me.

Not in the stupid version where anyone who becomes king automatically turns evil. That is too easy.

What interests me is what happens when someone gets enough power that fewer and fewer people can tell them no.

They start making exceptions for themselves. They start believing they understand things better than everybody else. They start thinking: Normally this would be wrong, but this situation is different.

Then: Normally nobody should have this authority, but I can be trusted with it.

Then eventually: I am the only one who can make this decision.

That is much more interesting to me.

And it gives Jarrock's future somewhere to come from.

At some point, Jarrock is apparently going to decide that removing the King and Queen from power is not enough. They need to be removed from existence.

That is a pretty extreme decision.

I do not want to just hand you the reason. I want Jarrock to live through things that make that future possible.

Maybe he sees people in power abuse it. Maybe he sees institutions protect the wrong person. Maybe he sees what happens when somebody who should have been stopped earlier is allowed to keep going. Maybe something completely different happens because of choices you make.

I do not know yet. That is the point.

I know where Jarrock eventually ends up. I do not know exactly how your Jarrock gets there. We get to find that out.

### And Then There Is Jill

The other half of that story may be even more important.

Jarrock kills Jill's parents. Then he takes Jill in.

That tells me that somewhere in Jarrock's life he needs to develop a very strong distinction between what your parents did and who you are.

Jill did not choose his parents.

If the King and Queen eventually become people Jarrock thinks have to die, that does not make their son guilty.

That is something I want Jarrock to have the chance to discover through play.

Not through some stupid NPC who walks up to him and says: Hello, Jarrock. Today's moral lesson is that children are not responsible for their parents. That would be terrible.

I want the campaign to give him experiences. Then you decide what they mean to him.

If someday we reach Jill's story and Jarrock can look at that kid after killing his parents and think: They had to die. You did nothing wrong. Then the backstory will feel like something that actually grew out of his life.

### Jarrock Also Changed How I Run D&D

Jarrock changed more than the story. He helped create one of my favorite house rules.

During the grick fight, Jarrock went down.

That made me think more about how being stabilized actually works at the table.

By the normal rules, once somebody is stable they can potentially spend a long time unconscious doing nothing. They are safe. But the player is sitting there watching everybody else play D&D.

I did not really like that.

So I came up with the rule we use now:

After you have been stabilized and have been down for three rounds, you can make a DC 15 Constitution check. If you succeed, you get back up with 1d4 hit points.

I like it. Three rounds is still a long time. DC 15 is not easy. 1d4 hit points is definitely not a full recovery. You can get punched right back down.

But at least the player has a chance to claw their way back into the fight.

That rule exists because of something that happened to your character.

That is the kind of thing I want these notes to remember. The players did not only change my plot. Sometimes you changed the rules of our table.

### Then We Actually Started Playing

Once the campaign started, the version in my notes immediately stopped being the only version that mattered.

That is just how D&D works.

I can prepare five pages. You guys can spend fifteen minutes trying to climb the wrong wall. That actually happened.

Pepper and Arunem wandered off. You guys spent time on things I assumed would take thirty seconds. Combat took longer than I expected.

The scavengers taught me things. The spiders taught me things. The grick definitely taught me things.

The timing of that first session was already changing the way I thought about encounter design.

Then Pepper murdered Kaleb.

### Kaleb Was Not My Plot

This is probably one of the clearest examples of how the players changed NCFA.

I did not sit down before Session 1 and think: I have a great long-term idea. Pepper is going to murder Kaleb, and then I can build an entire consequence arc out of it.

Pepper's player did that.

Once Kaleb was dead, though, I had to decide whether the world cared.

Of course it did.

Kaleb knew people. Kaleb had a wife. Kaleb had a daughter. The Council existed. There was a body.

The crime did not stop existing because the party left the room.

That one player decision created material I had never planned. And it is still creating material.

### Pepper Made Me Think Harder About Consequences

The fun part is that Pepper has also been ridiculously lucky.

Things keep going Pepper's way. Evidence gets destroyed. Rolls work. The player started saying things like: I HAVE TO GET AWAY WITH THIS MURDER.

That made me want the consequences to be fair.

I do not want to just declare: Surprise, you're caught because the DM says so.

If Pepper gets exposed, I want it to happen because something in the world makes sense. Somebody knows something. Somebody survives. Some piece of evidence still exists. The Council starts connecting things.

Whatever it is, I want it to feel earned.

That eventually led to the idea that Pepper could be forced into Council service: one unpaid job and then a series of paid jobs, with jail hanging over the situation if Pepper refuses.

But there was another thing I cared about even more.

That punishment should not decide Pepper's morality.

That is why Kaleb's wife and daughter are separate.

At some point, I want Kaleb's wife to come to Ledger House furious. She can tell Pepper: You murdered my husband. And then, despite hating Pepper, she can need a cleric to help her daughter.

Pepper can help. Pepper can refuse.

I do not want helping them to be part of the sentence. I do not want the Council to order it. I do not want the player to get mechanical credit for doing the decent thing.

Because if Pepper helps, I want it to mean something. And if Pepper says: Fuck you and your kid, that means something too.

That entire story exists because of a thing somebody at the table did that I never planned.

### Blackstone Changed Too

The same thing keeps happening.

At Blackstone, I could know who lived there. I could know who the captives were. I could know what the Iron Covenant was doing. I could prepare guards and rooms and clues.

Then you guys actually arrived.

You melted the bars off a rear window and went in that way.

Aaron killed a temple guard.

The guard captain got away. Another guard escaped too.

That matters. Somebody out there knows the party attacked Blackstone.

Pepper got the coded Iron Covenant ledger. You guys know Pepper has it.

Pepper had already taken the spyglass and compass from the satchel and burned the survey note.

Those choices changed the information state of the world.

The next session has to be based on the world that exists after you made those choices, not the world I had imagined before the session started.

That is increasingly how I think about campaign prep.

### The Council Stopped Being a Quest Board

Originally, the Council was an easy way to hand out reclamation jobs.

That is useful. It still does that.

But once you guys actually started interacting with the city, the Council could not just be five NPCs waiting in a room until you came back for your next assignment.

It had to act too.

The Council can send surveyors. It can reclaim territory. It can investigate a body. It can make political decisions. It can be wrong. It can be useful. It can do something while you are somewhere else.

That is part of the living-world thing again.

If you ignore the Council for three sessions, the Council does not go into stasis. They keep being the Council.

### The Old Module Was Becoming Less and Less Important

While all of this was happening, I was still technically using Ruins of Adventure as the big campaign framework.

But I kept changing it.

Different enemies. Different reasons for going places. Different clues. Different politics. Different rewards. Different NPCs. Different relationships. Sometimes completely different purposes for locations.

The more you guys played, the harder it became to justify saying: Well, this is what happens next because that is the next section in the old book.

Bellwether had its own momentum now.

The actual game was starting to tell me what should come next.

But I was still leaning on the published framework more than I really needed to.

Then I made Josric Pickle.

### Josric Pickle Changed NCFA Without Being in NCFA

Josric is not part of this campaign.

But making *Josric Pickle's Practical Course for Adventurers* had a huge effect on how I thought about this campaign.

With Josric, I was building the adventure myself.

I was designing rooms. I was throwing ideas away. I was changing puzzles. I was thinking about pacing. I was asking what the players might try.

And I realized that I had developed a design rule I really liked:

**Give the players a problem. Do not give them the solution.**

I can know some ways a room might be solved. I should know how the room works. I should know what happens if you screw it up.

But I do not need to decide: The only correct solution is to do these four steps in this order.

If you guys come up with something better, great. That is D&D.

Josric made me realize that I trusted myself enough to build those situations without needing somebody else's adventure underneath them.

That was the real reason I pivoted NCFA back toward homebrew.

### The Question Changed

Before Josric, I was still often thinking: How do I adapt the next part of Ruins of Adventure into Bellwether?

After Josric, the question became: **What should happen next in Bellwether?**

That sounds like a tiny difference. It is not.

One question assumes the old adventure is the roadmap and I am changing it.

The other assumes Bellwether is the roadmap and I can use the old adventure whenever it has something useful.

That is basically where Ruins of Adventure stopped being NCFA's spine.

It became a box of spare parts.

I can still steal from it. I probably will. But I no longer feel any obligation to follow it.

Josric is what gave me the confidence to make that change.

### Perrin Started Getting Better Once I Stopped Treating Him Like the Political Villain

Perrin Cardon changed a lot too.

At first, he was more straightforward: old family, political ambitions, claims in the reclaimed city, money, influence.

He could become a political antagonist. That worked. But it was not especially deep.

Perrin got more interesting when I stopped treating these two things as opposites: Perrin really does want Bellwether rebuilt, and Perrin really does want power.

Both can be true.

He can help people. He can support reclamation. He can sincerely believe the Cardon family was wronged. He can think Bellwether needs stronger leadership.

And he can increasingly decide that the person who should have that authority is Perrin Cardon.

That is much more interesting to me.

He does not need to secretly hate the city.

His problem is that the thing he thinks is good for the city and the thing that is good for Perrin can become harder and harder for him to tell apart.

### Tyren Went Through an Even Bigger Change

For a while, Tyren was basically a possessing intelligence behind the bigger supernatural plot.

That worked mechanically for the old campaign structure. But it always felt a little abstract.

The newer version finally gave him an identity I actually like.

Tyren is an **ancient emerald dragon who is imprisoned**.

That gives me a real answer for why somebody this powerful has to manipulate people instead of just doing everything himself.

He cannot.

So he needs people.

That immediately makes his relationship with Perrin more interesting.

Tyren did not create Perrin's ambition. He found it.

Perrin already had something Tyren could pull on.

That is how I want Tyren to work in general. He does not create everybody's flaws. He exploits what is already there.

That keeps him from becoming the explanation for the entire world.

### Tyren Is Not Secretly Responsible for Everything

This is important enough that I want it written down.

Tyren did not make Pepper kill Kaleb.

Tyren did not create every corrupt person in Bellwether.

Tyren did not cause every tragedy.

Tyren does not need to secretly be behind the Iron Covenant, every crime, every politician and every bad decision.

The world was already messy.

People already wanted things. People already hurt each other. People already wanted money. People already wanted power.

Tyren is dangerous because he is very good at finding those things and using them.

He is an exploiter.

He is not the explanation for humanity.

### The Strange Problem Tyren Has With You Guys

One of the things I really like about the new Tyren story is that the party is unusually difficult for him to influence.

Not because some prophecy says you are the Chosen Four. I do not really want that.

You are difficult for him because of who your characters already are.

Different people have different reasons. Some have trauma. Some have an unusually fixed sense of themselves. Some have moral convictions. Some just do not give Tyren the emotional handles he expects.

Brakkus has his own reasons. Bishop Brann has his own reasons.

The important part is that I do not want: Tyren can't control you because you're the protagonists.

I want: Tyren is starting to realize there is something unusual about these specific people.

That makes you important to him without making the entire universe revolve around you.

### I Also Do Not Want Tyren Playing Your Character for You

Tyren can get through sometimes.

But I do not want his power to become: Hand me your character sheet for twenty minutes.

I hate that.

The model I like is closer to *Command*.

One action.

One awful moment where something gets into your head and makes you do something.

Then you are you again.

It should be scary because it happened at all.

Not annoying because you stopped getting to play Jarrock.

### Jarrock's Future and the New Villain Structure Started Connecting Thematically

This is where some of the newer ideas started lining up in a way I really like.

Not because they are secretly connected in the plot.

Because they are asking related questions.

Milton believed freedom could justify control.

Perrin believes his vision of restoration can justify more authority.

Tyren literally manipulates other people's will.

And sometime in Jarrock's future, Jarrock decides that a King and Queen have crossed a line where removing them from office is not enough.

These are different people in different situations.

But all of them let us keep poking at the same ugly question: **What happens when someone has too much power?**

And then the harder one: **What are you justified in doing about it?**

I am not building an answer for you. That would defeat the point.

I am building situations where Jarrock is going to have reasons to develop an answer of his own.

### What I Have Learned About Writing the Campaign

At the beginning, I thought more in terms of plots.

Now I think much more in terms of situations.

Who is here? What do they want? What are they doing? What happens if nobody interferes?

Then you guys show up.

That is usually enough.

If I put the only important clue in one room and you never go into that room, I can move the information somewhere else if it makes sense.

Maybe somebody else knows it. Maybe it is in another document. Maybe you capture somebody. Maybe the place you decide to investigate turns out to contain the thing that moves the larger mystery forward.

I do not care whether you follow the route I imagined.

I care whether the world reacts honestly to the route you choose.

There is a difference between moving a clue and moving your choice.

I can move the clue.

I do not want to erase what you chose.

### You Guys Keep Writing Parts of the Campaign I Cannot Write

There are things I can prepare.

I can invent Bellwether. I can decide what Perrin wants. I can decide what Tyren is doing. I can know what the Iron Covenant believes. I can build Kellan's Well. I can decide what the Council is arguing about.

What I cannot write is the part where four actual people sit down and start making decisions.

I did not write Pepper's murder.

I did not write Pepper's ridiculous luck afterward.

I did not write Aaron killing the temple guard.

I did not write the captain escaping.

I did not write Jarrock going down against the grick.

I did not write Pepper getting back up on a natural 20.

I did not write the fifteen minutes of screwing around with the wall.

Those are now part of NCFA anyway.

And they are not filler.

They change what I do next.

That means this campaign belongs to all of us in a way a novel never could.

I own the world behind the screen.

You guys own your characters.

What actually happens belongs somewhere in the middle.

### The Next Big Pivot: I Quit Following the Reclamation Campaign

Even after Josric convinced me I could write my own adventures, part of my plan was still built around the idea that we would keep reclaiming sections of Bellwether. Different locations, different problems, increasingly powerful enemies. That was useful, but I was beginning to run into the same problem again: I was thinking about what the next chapter should be instead of what the people in Bellwether would actually do.

I wanted a bigger direction for the campaign. Not necessarily a bigger villain. I wanted the kind of change that would make everybody in the city have an opinion, even the people who would never pick up a sword.

Then I started thinking about Jarrock's future again. We already knew from Jill's story that Jarrock would eventually kill a King and Queen and take their child in. What was missing was the life Jarrock would have to live before that decision made any sense.

So I stopped thinking of the next adventure as another district to clear. I decided we were going to have a **Crown adventure**. Bellwether was going to go from a free city to a city ruled by a monarchy.

That was another actual pivot, not just a new subplot. *Ruins of Adventure* had already become a box of spare parts. Now the reclamation campaign itself was going to give way to something else. I would keep the places, people, maps and problems that still made sense, but I wasn't going to pretend we had to finish the old chapter order.

I did not decide what Jarrock would do about the Crown. I decided to give him a world where the question would eventually matter.

### The Most Ridiculous King and Queen I Could Take Seriously

I also wanted the new royalty to be **comically evil**. Not secretly misunderstood. Not complicated villains who just needed somebody to explain their childhood trauma. I wanted the sort of King and Queen who could say something absolutely monstrous with complete confidence that they were being generous.

The trick is that nobody inside the world should be playing it as a joke. If an official announces that the citizens have been graciously relieved of their freedom and must now pay for the privilege, the official means it. The guards mean it. And the people who have to live under that decree can't just laugh it off.

That contrast is funny to me. A ridiculous premise taken seriously is a lot more interesting than everybody making jokes about how ridiculous it is.

The Crown also cannot explain everything wrong with Bellwether. People were cheating, stealing, protecting their families and abusing power before the royal banners showed up. Some people will do better under the Crown. Some will be crushed by it. Some will try to get on with their day.

There are still ordinary lives going on. I don't want Bellwether to stop being Bellwether just because the biggest people in it have changed.

### The Council Sold the City While You Were Doing Its Job

Here is the part of the pivot I liked best: I didn't need to have an army march through the gates or make the King and Queen conquer the place.

The Council had already negotiated away Bellwether's independence. They had given you a legitimate job at Kellan's Well, and while you were out doing it, the city was being transferred to the Crown. The people running the city were allowed to make their own decisions while the party was elsewhere, and some of those decisions were awful.

I thought that was more interesting than telling the players they had somehow caused the takeover. You didn't cause it. You weren't even consulted. And once you got home, you would have to decide what to do about it.

Koven House would become the Crown's headquarters. The Council's old role as the organization handing out jobs was finished. Bishop Brann would remain in Bellwether. Brakkus would still have Ledger House. And the people who had spent the last few weeks rebuilding a free city would suddenly have to figure out who owned their work.

I also had an entirely different reason for wanting the change. Kaleb's death was still following Pepper around. The Council's punishment was one unpaid job, then four paid jobs. The new regime would complicate all that, and Kaleb's wife would become an indentured servant of the Crown. His adopted brother, Tulcornix Avemvir, could bring the party the news that she needed help.

That doesn't mean Pepper has to save her. It means the world did something with the consequences of Kaleb's death, and the next decision belongs to the players.

### Noren Told You Before Bellwether Could

Before we sat down for Kellan's Well, I decided Noren was in charge of the Iron Covenant and was connected enough as a criminal to know what the Council had arranged. I meant to update the adventure notes but couldn't get the right file in front of me.

That turned out to matter. When Noren needed to save his own skin, he told the party the truth about the Crown. He was trying to make you question what you were doing and whom you were doing it for.

So the big reveal changed shape. Instead of learning for the first time when you walked through the city gates, you could come home knowing what a criminal had claimed and find out whether he was telling the truth.

I like that better than an NPC giving a speech about what has happened. Noren had his own reasons to talk. You had your own reasons to doubt him. Nobody needed to deliver an exposition dump because the next chapter was supposed to start.

More importantly, I didn't have to protect a scripted surprise by pretending Noren was too stupid or too poorly connected to know something he reasonably would have known.

### What Kellan's Well Actually Looked Like When You Played It

The funny thing about Kellan's Well is that you guys solved some of the hardest problems without doing what I expected. You used *Tasha's Hideous Laughter* to stop the runner before the alarm went off. You used *Fog Cloud* in the Bow Gallery so the Rangers couldn't see you crossing the room.

Aaron read the surface thoughts of one of the Rangers. I got to have the Ranger say one thing, then tell Aaron what he was actually thinking. His words and his thoughts didn't match, and you learned something useful about where Noren was.

Then Arunem broke through the ceiling to get around Noren and his Veteran. That is not something I could have written as the one right way to get into that fight. It worked because he looked at the place as an actual place, not just squares on a map.

Pepper, meanwhile, had decided that if the Council wasn't paying her, she was going to find her own compensation. She searched enemy barrels, bodies and chests, and at one point she went for a chest instead of helping fight. Pepper is a cleric. That is what made the choice particularly funny.

And she was still angry with Jarrock for telling the Council about Kaleb. So she was refusing to heal him. Jarrock's response was basically, 'I was just keeping it real.' Both players were laughing. I couldn't have scripted their argument, and I wouldn't want to.

You also decided Jarrock was going to get drunk before work. Brakkus gave him a chance to change his mind, and you stayed with the decision. The disadvantages that came with it didn't make you stop playing Jarrock. They became part of playing him.

I have been saying I want the decisions to matter. That session gave me a whole lot of examples of what I mean.

### The Things You Are Learning Without Me Giving a Lesson

Something else happened in those sessions that I didn't expect to enjoy as much as I do: I'm watching you work through consequences that are a lot closer to real life than hit points and treasure.

Jarrock told the truth about Pepper. I think there was a good reason to do it. That didn't mean Pepper had to be happy with him afterward. You got to see that you can do what you think is right and still have somebody be angry at you.

You also got to choose something fun for Jarrock and see that being drunk made doing his job harder. I didn't have to announce that drinking has consequences. Jarrock made the choice and had to deal with the result.

I'm not trying to turn our Sunday game into a lecture. I want you to have room to decide what Jarrock believes, make mistakes, defend your choices, and occasionally discover that the consequences aren't quite what you expected.

And I get to see that happen while we're all having fun. That's a pretty great thing to share with my son.

### Years of Fun Sundays

After Kellan's Well, the players started talking about wanting to reach level 20. I pointed out that a campaign lasting that long could take years.

The response was, 'We'd have something fun to do on Sundays.'

I liked that more than somebody telling me I had built a good encounter. It meant you weren't just waiting to see how the next fight turned out. You could imagine us doing this together for a long time.

I'm still figuring out how to be a better DM. I want to build things that are ready to run, and I want enough freedom to change them when you guys do something I couldn't predict. Those goals aren't opposites. That's the job.

We may or may not ever reach level 20. I don't need that answer right now. The part I don't want to forget is that you guys thought years of Sundays sounded like a good idea.

### Why I Am Keeping These Notes

This is the first full campaign I have ever run for you.

That matters to me.

I do not just want to keep a record of Session 1: this happened. Session 2: this happened. Session 3: this happened.

I want you to be able to see the campaign changing while we played it.

I want you to know that his name was Osric Vale for a while, that the Silent Vault became the Quiet Vault, and that I once had an entire different Session 1 built around getting Milton and Tomas out of it.

I want you to know that Calder Vane and the Harrow Collective were once supposed to be the villains you spent half the campaign fighting, and that even winning against them was not going to end the story.

I want you to know that Osric existed before Milton.

I want you to know what the Silent Vault was supposed to be.

I want you to know that I went digging through old D&D adventures because I was trying to figure out the best way to build the kind of campaign I wanted.

I want you to know why Under Illefarn almost became the beginning.

Why the Slave Lords had ideas I liked but the wrong structure.

Why Bloodstone was too big too soon.

Why Princes of the Apocalypse almost worked but would have made the campaign about something I did not actually care about enough.

Why Ruins of Adventure finally gave me the structure I needed.

Why Brakkus is standing behind the bar at Ledger House instead of still being my character at Hall of Heroes.

Why Jarrock's future with Jill started changing the themes I wanted to explore.

Why Jarrock going down in one fight changed a rule we use at the table.

Why Pepper murdering one guy turned into something I may still be dealing with twenty sessions later.

Why Josric Pickle, who is not even in this campaign, changed how I thought about writing it.

Why I eventually stopped asking what the old adventure said should happen next.

Why Perrin became more complicated.

Why Tyren became a dragon.

Why I stopped treating reclamation as the whole campaign and turned it into a Crown adventure.

Why I wanted the King and Queen to be ridiculous while the people who lived under them took it seriously.

Why Noren warned you about the takeover before you even got back to the city.

Why I remember a fog cloud, a disabled runner, somebody breaking through the ceiling, and Pepper choosing a chest over a fight.

Why I was so happy to hear that years of playing D&D sounded like fun Sundays.

And why there are still going to be people in Bellwether doing completely ordinary things that have absolutely nothing to do with any of them.

Because that is the part I think will be easy to lose later.

When a campaign is finished, everything tends to look intentional.

It starts looking like the story was always headed exactly where it ended.

It was not.

This thing has already changed a ridiculous number of times.

It is going to change again.

Some of those changes will come from me.

Some will come from the other players.

Some will come from a die hitting the table.

And some will come from you deciding that Jarrock does something I never considered.

That is not you messing up my story.

**That is how we are making it.**
